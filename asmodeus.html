In one paragraph

A stream whose law changes, then comes back. Asmodeus keeps one small table per law and a softmax over them, so a returning law is recalled, not re-learned. The question: When a stream switches between a few laws and each law returns, does keeping one small model per law, and a softmax that routes between them, beat learners that adapt or read the regime from context? The headline result: 11×, lower regret against an oracle than the best owned online learner, on streams of 4 recurring laws, 10 fresh seeds: 0.0277 vs 0.3056 nats per token. Control: the best owned learner (BOCPD), tuned on separate seeds; when no law ever returns, the era bank costs 1.18× BOCPD (0.0186 vs 0.0159). Code: teerthsharma/Asmodeus on GitHub.

What it is

Asmodeus came out of resolvent and caustic together.

The name is a demon’s twice over. The repository’s tagline calls it a time traveller on a chronal fluid, and its epigraph is the folklore of Asmodeus, king of the demons, whose legion is nowhere until it is called by name; in the repository’s vocabulary the decider is the time traveller, the stream is the fluid, and the decider’s weight vector is the fluid’s density. The design comes from a different demon, the one Pierre-Simon Laplace described in 1814 in his Essai philosophique sur les probabilités: an intellect that knew the exact state of the world at one instant could read the past and the future from it. Asmodeus borrows the shape of that idea and none of its omniscience. It keeps a table of counts for each earlier stretch of a stream, and when the present needs what an earlier stretch knew, it goes back to that table. The tallies are exact, but which symbols belong to which stretch is an estimate that a later re-address can revise. That is why it jumps between eras. Everything after this paragraph says what is computed instead of what it is called.

The data is a stream of symbols from a finite alphabet. For a stretch of time one law generates it, here a table of next-symbol probabilities given the previous symbol. Then the law changes, and some time later an earlier law comes back. A learner that tracks such a stream by forgetting, with an exponential decay or a change-point detector, has to learn a returning law from scratch every time it returns. A learner that reads the law from its context window cannot use anything older than the window. Asmodeus keeps instead one count table per law, called an era, and a vector of weights over the eras. The weights multiply each new symbol’s likelihood under each era and renormalise; they drift along an era-to-era kernel learned from the jumps already seen; a detector whose false-alarm rate is bounded by Ville’s inequality opens a new era when the current one stops fitting; and two eras that turn out to hold the same law are merged. Old eras are not overwritten: only symbols credited after the estimated change time can be moved out of one.

The repository describes the target as data that “is not one distribution” but a stream whose governing law changes and often comes back. Every measurement before the October 2026 update is on synthetic streams of exactly that shape: sixteen symbols, first-order laws, 20,000 symbols per stream; the one real-text result, a small pilot, is in that section. The roadmap’s next two stages, a batched GPU engine and a real-text pilot, had no code at the pinned commit 4b768f7, where the repository had no CUDA source and no eralm package; a torch engine and the pilot arrived afterwards. At the pinned commit what exists is a NumPy reference implementation, a set of controls, the Lean proofs, and a fluid-dynamics line (FCPA), from which the era bank grew, that this essay mentions only where it supplies a lemma.

What it can do

All numbers below are regret against an oracle, in nats per symbol: the mean log-loss of a method minus the mean log-loss of an oracle that is told the true law at every position and counts symbols per true law with the same Dirichlet prior (a = 0.2). Lower is better. Every result is on evaluation seeds that no earlier round used for tuning. The repository says each pass-or-fail bar was written down before its result existed, and records a sha256 for each bar file in research/e0/FREEZE.txt; the bar files for E0b to E1 and the E0 program file all hash to the recorded values once line endings are normalised. How far the order of events can be checked is stated under Limitations.

The gain is memory, not detection speed. Take the first 100 symbols of every segment whose law was seen earlier in the stream, which is where a learner that forgot has to start again. There the era bank is 0.0839 nats above the oracle, against 0.6930 for BOCPD and 0.8399 for exponential decay. The era bank also did not invent laws that were not there: on streams with one law and no switch it opened 0 extra eras over 10 seeds, and on the recurring streams it ended with exactly 4 eras on 8 of 10 seeds and 5 on the other two.

The cost of this memory appears where nothing is worth remembering:

The era bank is therefore not free when laws never repeat, and in that regime it ends with 44 to 64 eras per stream. It is close to free: the Lean bound below says the overhead per symbol is at most about α/2 for the decider that won, which is 0.0015 at α = 0.003.

Which way to move the weights

Inside the era bank, the decider has a choice of how its weight vector moves between symbols. Seven were run on the same bank, tuned on seeds that evaluation did not use, and scored on fresh seeds 40 to 49 as the mean regret over four stream types (recurring, cyclic, fast-cyclic, no recurrence). The one that moves weight along a learned era-to-era kernel, called MOM, won:

The gain is 7% on the cyclic stream, 6% on the fast cyclic one, 8% on the no-recurrence one and 2% on the recurring stream where the next law is chosen uniformly. It is not confined to streams where the eras follow a path: it is highest on the stream with no recurrence at all. A rule that answers with the single top expert alone whenever its weight is at least 0.99 scores 0.0516, the same as SOFT, and it computed only that one expert on 45.8% of the symbols, averaged over the four scored stream types: 53% on the recurring stream, 56% on the cyclic, 64% on the fast cyclic and 10% where nothing returns. The repository reads this as a compute result: on those symbols a prediction needs one table, not the blend.

Against models that read the law from context

The strongest competitor to a stored era is a network that infers the law from the preceding symbols. Four were built: two small recurrent networks trained online during the stream, one recurrent network meta-trained on 50 million symbols of streams from the same generator family, and one causal transformer with 862,992 parameters and a 512-symbol context, meta-trained the same way. The meta-trained arms saw the generator family and the era bank did not, a choice made on purpose to favour them.

The ratio of those two means on the recurring stream is 17.0. The repository’s own text says 15; that is the ratio on the worst of the ten seeds (14.9), and the per-seed ratio averages 17. The preregistered bar, the era bank at no more than half of the best neural arm, held on 10 of 10 seeds for both the recurring stream and the fast-cyclic one. The explanation the repository gives is memory: each law is a fresh 16 by 16 table, the transformer sees 512 symbols and a fast-cyclic segment is about 100 symbols long, so it cannot estimate a table, while the era bank keeps each table for the whole stream.

A worked merge

Merging is a decision between two count tables, so it can be shown on two. Take the first recurring bed’s laws and draw 64 transitions from law 0 twice, and 64 from law 1. The log of the Dirichlet–multinomial Bayes factor for fusing a table with another is +29.1 nats for the two draws of law 0 and −19.2 nats for law 0 against law 1. At 200 transitions each the same pair gives +79.1 and −108.5; at 400, +92.9 and −286.2. A positive value merges. The same-law value grows slowly and the different-law value grows linearly in the evidence, which is the shape Lean lemma L39c proves in general.

No upstream pull request came out of Asmodeus; this blog’s lineage table lists none for it.

October 2026: from toy streams to text

The era bank now runs as a language model, the Era LM. A small recurrent network with a fixed-size state predicts the next token, and next to it sits a bank of eras, each a closed-form ridge readout on the network’s features, because a table of counts does not carry over to words. A cache of the last 512 tokens is a further expert. The decider described above, MOM with α=0.003\alpha = 0.003α=0.003, mixes the experts token by token. The batched torch engine under the era bank is tested against the NumPy reference: its 11 tests pass, and the ones that compare the bank, the decider’s weights and the BOCPD probabilities with the reference hold to 10−1010^{-10}10−10 (zero/tests/test_eralm_engine.py:56, 64, 76). The engine is a torch program that runs on the GPU; the repository still has no CUDA source file.

The first real-text pilot

Before this update every number in this essay was measured on a stream built to switch laws. The pilot is a first measurement on text that was not. It trains on the first 1,000,000 tokens of TinyStories (the corpus and the 4,096-token vocabulary are pinned by hash), and scores the next-token loss in nats on the first 100,000 tokens of the held-out split. Context is 128 tokens, batch 64, AdamW at 0.003, 1500 steps, on one RTX 4060 Laptop GPU. Four controls were built to the same size, 5,820,416 to 5,945,784 parameters against the Era LM’s 5,829,736: a GPT-style transformer, a state-space model, a mixture of four experts with one expert per token, and a linear-attention transformer. An order-3 Jelinek–Mercer n-gram fitted to the same tokens is the floor.

The two Era LM seeds end at 3.1003 and 3.0818, which is 0.5319 and 0.5503 nats below the n-gram. The state-space model ends at 3.1298 and 3.1020, and the GPT at 3.8830 and 4.2927. Inside the Era LM, the network alone scores 3.1058 and 3.0886 on the same two seeds, so the bank takes 0.0055 and 0.0068 nats off the loss: it helps on both seeds. The MoE ends at 3.0407 and 3.3786 over its two seeds. That seed spread of about 0.34 is larger than the gap between the arms, so two seeds do not order the MoE against the Era LM. Linear attention was not re-run in this pilot.

A second alarm for the detector

The detector that opens new eras (described under Birth, below) waits until the segment holds 64 transitions of evidence before it can fire. The engine now carries a second alarm beside it: a Shiryaev–Roberts statistic (Shiryaev, 1963; Roberts, 1966) that compares the present stretch with the eras already stored and fires when its logarithm passes ln⁡104\ln 10^4ln104, with no waiting period. It sits behind a flag, detector="sr", and the default stays "legacy", the alarm described under Birth, so the numbers elsewhere in this essay do not move (zero/eralm/engine.py:18-19, 114-124, 194-202). The 11 engine tests above pass with the flag in the code.

The next leap: a read that does not commute

The repository’s other model family keeps a small quantum-style state and reads it with the Born rule (QF-LM). In its default form every one-step prediction is a mixture of NNN rows of one table (zero/qflm/model.py:113-116), so what it can say about the next symbol is limited by NNN. A test bed was built to measure the limit: pairs (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) over 16 symbols with P(a,b)∝(a−b)2P(a, b) \propto (a - b)^2P(a,b)∝(a−b)2, read with N=2N = 2N=2.

The working explanation, taken from the code, is that a mixture of rows has a floor on this law. The arm that would test it replaces the commuting read with a measurement that has more than one Kraus operator, acting on a locally purified state; locally purified states are treated in Glasser, Sweke, Pancotti, Eisert and Cirac (arXiv:1907.03741). What is established is the gap and the bar. The arm that closes it has not been run.

Where this could help

The property measured in this essay is a system whose rules switch and then come back, where relearning a rule each time is expensive: a swarm that changes task, a network that changes regime. Those settings are collected as opportunity cards, not results, at blog.teerth.dev/asmodeus. Each card names what in its domain matches the property and what has not been measured.

The era

DefinitionEra

An era is an order-1 count table Ck∈NV×VC_k \in \mathbb{N}^{V \times V}Ck​∈NV×V together with a Dirichlet prior. With V=16V = 16V=16 symbols and prior weight a=0.2a = 0.2a=0.2, the probability the era gives symbol xxx after symbol x′x'x′ is

The equation is the whole model of one law. Nothing is learned by gradient; the table is a tally, and that is what makes it cheap to keep and exact to merge.

In code, the learned move is a short method:

def share(self):
    n_e = len(self.w) - self.n_tail
    out = (1 - self.alpha) * self.w + 0.5 * self.alpha / len(self.w)  # half the shared mass: uniform
    moved = 0.5 * self.alpha * self.w  # other half: era mass travels along Q, tail mass stays
    if n_e > 1:

        np.fill_diagonal(Q, 0.0)

    return out + moved

What’s new in it

Four things are done differently from the usual approach, each stated against the approach by name.

The expert set is not fixed. Fixed share (Herbster and Warmuth) tracks the best expert in a pool given in advance. Here the pool is a bank of count tables that grows by birth and shrinks by merge while the stream runs, and the regret bound in the previous section holds for that changing pool.

Old laws are kept. The usual online learner for a changing stream, exponential decay or a change-point detector such as BOCPD, lets the past fade or down-weights it after a change. The era bank keeps every table. On the symbols right after a return that is the difference between 0.0839 and 0.6930 nats of excess regret, and the Lean tree proves that tokens already settled in an era are never lost from it, while a decayed table keeps λL\lambda^LλL of its counts after LLL steps away.

The weights learn the order of the laws. Fixed share moves weight with no idea of which law tends to follow which. The learned kernel Q~\tilde QQ~​ is the count of observed jumps, and the decider that uses it is 6 to 7% better on the cyclic streams; it is also 8% better on the stream with no recurrence, so the gain cannot be put down to the order of the laws alone.

Answering with one table. When one expert has at least 0.99 of the weight, the decider can answer with that expert alone. Averaged over four stream types that was possible on 45.8% of symbols with no measurable loss.

The comparison with networks that read the law from context sits in the second measured block above: a stored table is a memory the length of the stream, and a context window is not.

What no one else built

I compared Asmodeus against the closest work I could find. None of its ingredients is new, and the repository says as much about fixed share, BOCPD, the e-detector and the hidden-Markov view. Each entry names the work and the concrete difference.

Fixed share and tracking the best expert. Herbster and Warmuth, Tracking the Best Expert (Machine Learning 32, 1998) proves regret bounds against the best sequence of experts with a bounded number of switches, for a fixed expert set. It is the incumbent decider here (SOFT). The difference is the changing pool: births, merges and a learned jump kernel, and a proved bound for that.

Mixing past posteriors. Bousquet and Warmuth, Tracking a Small Set of Experts by Mixing Past Posteriors (JMLR 3, 2002) is the closest prior treatment of experts that return: when the best expert shifts back and forth inside a small pool of mmm experts, its bounds pay once to identify the pool and then log⁡m\log mlogm per new section, instead of log⁡n\log nlogn per section for a pool of nnn. Its experts are given. In Asmodeus the experts are count tables created and fused during the run, and the return path is a learned kernel over them.

Expert advice as a hidden Markov model. Koolen and de Rooij, Combining Expert Advice Efficiently (COLT 2008) shows that switching schemes for expert advice, fixed share among them, can be written as hidden Markov models whose standard algorithms compute the weights. Lean lemma L41 proves the same identification for MOM. What the repository adds is that the transition matrix is not chosen but estimated from the system’s own jumps, and the pool changes over time.

Change-point detection. Adams and MacKay, Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection (arXiv:0710.3742) maintains a posterior over how long the current regime has lasted. It is both the strongest owned baseline and one of the three pool members here. The difference is the bank: BOCPD’s posterior is over run lengths, so after a change the earlier regime survives only as down-weighted old run lengths (the code caps them at 1,500 and keeps prefix counts over the whole stream), while the era bank holds each earlier law as a table of its own.

E-detectors. Shin, Ramdas and Rinaldo, E-detectors: a nonparametric framework for sequential change detection (arXiv:2203.03532) gives the mixture detector used for births, and Ramdas, Grünwald, Vovk and Shafer, Game-theoretic statistics and safe anytime-valid inference (arXiv:2210.01948) is a survey of the guarantee (Ville’s inequality) it rests on. The difference is the use: the detector’s alarm drives an address step that chooses between jumping to an old era and opening a new one, with eras estimated online. The false-alarm bound is proved for the detector in Lean; that it survives with estimated eras is measured here (0 extra eras on 10 null streams), not proved.

Nonparametric regime-switching models. Fox, Sudderth, Jordan and Willsky, A sticky HDP-HMM with application to speaker diarization (arXiv:0905.2592) augments a hierarchical-Dirichlet-process hidden Markov model, which has an unknown number of states, so that the switching rate is controlled; it is a Bayesian nonparametric answer to “an unknown number of regimes”. It is fitted with a sampling algorithm that resamples the whole state sequence. Asmodeus replaces the posterior with a tally and a detector and proves a regret bound instead, and the repository does not run an HDP-HMM baseline, so how the two compare on these streams is not measured.

Gated mixtures of experts. Shazeer et al., The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer (arXiv:1701.06538) routes each input to a few experts with a learned gating network and a load-balancing loss. Here the router is a Bayes filter over experts with no gradient-learned gate, and the experts are count tables, not networks. The roadmap names the same failure that the sparse layers needed to fix, one expert taking all the weight. The torch engine now monitors era usage and flags a collapse when one era holds more than 90% of the era weight (zero/eralm/model.py:157-162); whether it fires in the text pilot below is not reported.

Learning in context. Garg, Tsipras, Liang and Valiant, What Can Transformers Learn In-Context? (arXiv:2208.01066) trains transformers to infer a function from examples in the prompt. The meta-trained transformer in the comparison above is this kind of model, at 0.86M parameters. The result compares a stored table against a context window of 512 symbols at that size; it does not say anything about larger models.

Laplace’s demon. The idea in Laplace’s essay is the inspiration named in the first section, not prior work that Asmodeus implements. Asmodeus is a probabilistic filter over count tables, not a determinism claim.

What survives the comparison is the combination. Count-table eras born under a Ville-bounded detector and fused by a Bayes factor that is the same rule as the address step, a weight vector that moves along a kernel learned from its own jumps, and a regret bound for the whole growing, merging bank checked in Lean. I make no claim beyond that combination, and none about real data beyond the small pilot reported below.

Limitations

The evidence for the era mechanism is synthetic apart from one small real-text pilot, the new detector is not yet the default, and the Lean proofs are about an abstract filter, not about the Python. Here is what was withdrawn and what is not covered, as the repository and a re-check of it state them.

What failed7 of 7 hypotheses withdrawn

  • Withdrawn: C3 in its first form: the era count tracks the number of laws

    Killed by: E0 ended with 7–11 eras and E0b with 4–8 for 4 laws; restored in E0c by a global Bayes-factor merge and a 64-transition evidence wait, exactly 4 eras on 8 of 10 seeds (research/VERDICT_E0.md:11, 19; e0/RESULTS_E0c.md:16, 44)

  • Withdrawn: Tuning every decider at alpha = 0.01

    Killed by: failed the no-recurrence gate for five of six deciders; the Lean bound shows the gate is guaranteed only for alpha <= 0.0039 (fixed share) or 0.0078 (MOM) (research/VERDICT_TOURNAMENT.md:27; lean/ERA_LEMMAS.md:57-60)

  • Withdrawn: A closed-timelike-curve fixed point as the decider's answer

    Killed by: 0.0520 against fixed share's 0.0515 and failing the no-recurrence gate; a Lean lemma (L32) had predicted it adds nothing (e0/RESULTS_E0e.md:9, README.md:343)

  • Withdrawn: Tempered-likelihood and temperature deciders

    Killed by: 0.0523 and 0.0516, no gain over fixed share; the temperature one also fails the gate (e0/RESULTS_E0e.md:10, 12)

  • Withdrawn: The chronal-fluid density as the data's own law density (Addendum 1)

    Killed by: the inventor's notes, read again, put it on the decider's weight vector; withdrawn in Addendum 2 (research/PROGRAM.md:120)

  • Withdrawn: No forgetting, stated without a condition

    Killed by: a re-address removes unsettled tokens from their era; counterexample at lean/ERA_LEMMAS.md:132-134, the proved form covers settled tokens only

  • Withdrawn: A linear era model beats the persistence forecast at 50 steps on the Navier–Stokes stream

    Killed by: stated as the open blocker of the fluid line (README.md:361, 442); not re-derived here

What the repository does not contain. At the pinned commit there is no CUDA source, no .cu file and no eralm package, and stage 6, the batched GPU engine with a parallel-scan filter, and stage 7, the real-text pilot, are marked not started. Since then the torch engine has landed in zero/eralm/ (0a71f88); it runs on the GPU through torch, there is still no .cu file, the stage-6 throughput benchmark is pending, and the pilot has a first result. The README badge names a CUDA card; the neural controls in the first comparison ran on a CPU, per the repository’s own amendment. Everything measured is a first-order Markov source on 16 symbols, built to favour eras; the oracle and every count model share the generating prior, and the tuned detector, window and evidence settings sit on the edges of their grids.

Places where the README and the code differ. Seven were found, and the essay uses the code’s value in each.

  • The README says the era bank has 15 times lower regret than the transformer. The per-seed worst case is 14.9 and the ratio of means is 17.0.
  • The README says re-entering a seen regime costs 0.70 nats for BOCPD and 0.86 for exponential decay; those are the second-round (E0b) values, 0.7014 and 0.8624; the first round gives 0.6933 and 0.8246. The values in the round the essay reports (E0c) are 0.6930 and 0.8399.
  • The README says MOM’s gain is largest where the eras follow a path. Its own table has the no-recurrence stream highest, at 8%, ahead of 7% and 6% on the cyclic streams.
  • The README says the decider keeps no raw past, only its weights and the eras’ sufficient statistics. The reference code also keeps the last 4,000 credited transitions, so that an alarm can take the new segment out of the eras it was credited to, a window of the last 32 symbols, and a BOCPD expert that reads running counts over the whole stream so far. That is the design intent not yet met by the reference code.
  • The README says merging happens only after an era holds 64 transitions of evidence. In the code the 64 delays the address decision for the segment after an alarm, and the pairwise merge that follows looks at every pair of eras whatever its size.
  • The README says 40 Lean files, and that the learned-kernel rate and a finite-sample merge threshold are open. The tree has 43 lemma modules; three of them, L39d_MergeThreshold, L41b_LearnedKernel and L42_ShippedFilter, postdate that text and prove a corrected merge threshold with a false-merge probability bound, a regret bound for the learned kernel, and exactness of the log-space filter.
  • The README calls the birth lemma (L23) non-destructive birth of an era. The Lean file L23_NonDestructiveBirth.lean is the quantum-effect version, with the old probabilities unchanged by definition of the new family. The count-table version is count_birth_old_unchanged in L40_NoForgetting.lean, and it also holds by definition of the model of a birth. The non-trivial statement is no_forgetting.

Proof and code. The Lean proofs are about an abstract run: rates in range, positive likelihoods, a pool-size bound and a comparator inside the pool are its hypotheses, and the Python is related to it by a check recorded in ERA_LEMMAS.md (the bound held on 18 runs); I found no script or test in the repository that reproduces that check. The merge threshold in the shipped code is log⁡BF>0\log \mathrm{BF} > 0logBF>0, which the repository’s own lemma threshold_one_vacuous shows carries no false-merge guarantee, and the corrected threshold proved in L39d is not what the code uses. The tuned minimum evidence of 64 transitions is not derived.

What was and was not run. Re-run for this essay on Windows 11 with Python 3.11: the era reference tests (16 passed), the E1 harness tests (5 passed), the FCPA estimator tests (406 passed, 4 skipped, 2 xfailed) and the repository’s zero/tests directory (905 passed, 3 failed, 3 skipped, 1 xfailed). Of the three failures, one is a test that writes a file name containing a colon, which Windows rejects, and two are assertions in test_nerve_persistence.py of the older chart-atlas line; none touches the era code. The E0c experiment and the SOFT, MOM and HARD part of E0e were run end to end and match the committed tables; the Lean modules were compiled as described above, and lake build itself was not run, because it needs a Mathlib checkout that the repository does not vendor. The neural controls were not re-trained; they take about an hour and a half. The numbers are recorded in the ledger. Preregistration order is not shown by git history for the first five rounds: the frozen bar files for E0b to E0e and their results landed in one commit, so the order rests on the timestamps in FREEZE.txt. For the neural round the commit that added the frozen bars is dated 1 hour 21 minutes before the commit that added the results. The rounds from E0 to E1 are all dated within about two and a half hours.

What Laplace’s demon is and Asmodeus is not. The demon knows the state of everything and reads past and future from it exactly. Asmodeus holds counts of first-order transitions on a 16-symbol alphabet, with a Dirichlet prior, so each era is a smoothed frequency table and nothing more. Its prediction is a probability for the next symbol, never a certain one. The record of the past is also not perfect: settled tokens are never lost from an era, but a re-address can move tokens that came after the estimated change time. Nothing in the repository claims determinism or perfect recall, and the code does not provide either.

Scale. The best context reader was 0.86M parameters; a meta-trained recurrent network never learned the task in context under the frozen recipe, so it is a weak competitor; a larger transformer or a longer context could close the gap, and the repository assigns that comparison to a later phase. The comparison with small GPTs at equal compute, the scaling study and the downstream benchmarks (stages 8 to 13) are not started.

What the October 2026 additions do not show.

  • Scope of the pilot. One subset, 1500 steps, 2 seeds. The Era LM’s lead over the SSM, 0.0295 and 0.0202 nats, is about the size of the seed-to-seed spread of the SSM (0.0278) and of the Era LM itself (0.0184), so two seeds do not make the ordering a result, and the MoE’s two seeds, 3.0407 and 3.3786, straddle the Era LM’s. The GPT’s two seeds differ by 0.41 nats (3.883 and 4.293) and both finish above the n-gram (3.632): it is under-tuned at this budget, and the gap to it is not a claim about transformers in general.
  • Arms that are missing or not comparable. The first linear-attention run went to NaN from step 750 under bf16; the cause, a feature map that is exactly zero in bf16, is fixed and the re-run has not finished, so no loss is reported. The MoE ran in fp32, because its router code does not run under bf16 autocast, and every other arm in bf16. The Asmodeus-native QF-LM arm ran at context 32 for 300 steps with 287,680 parameters and ended at 7.0217; it is not comparable, and a matched-budget run was not done because its cost, 857 tokens per second, did not fit.
  • What the bank adds, and what it costs. On this text the era bank takes 0.0055 and 0.0068 nats, about 0.2%, off the loss of the network it sits on; the large gains in this essay remain on streams built to switch laws and return. The Era LM trains at 27,952 and 31,437 tokens per second against 116,560 and 118,785 for the SSM and 270,661 and 278,536 for the GPT on the same GPU, and the stage-6 throughput benchmark is pending.
  • The new alarm is not the default. It is faster, and it also fires more often: 26 to 34 switches to a stored era per 1,200-token stream against 16 or 17. On the same six cyclic streams it ends with 9 to 13 eras and no fold, against 8 to 11 for the old alarm and a repair bar of 4; on eight one-law streams it ends with 5 to 10 eras where the old alarm ends with 1 (3 on seed 0); on a 4-symbol control it ends with more eras than the old alarm on 4 of 6 seeds. Three repair bars written for it are red, so the default stays on the old alarm.
  • The next leap is a target. The gap of 0.0386 nats per pair is measured on one constructed bed; the 0.005 bar is registered before the arm exists, and the arm is not built.
  • Where the files are. The pilot’s result files and the detector edit are in the project’s working tree and are published with the next push; the claims above are made at that checkout, not at the pinned commit.
